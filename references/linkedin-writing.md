# LinkedIn writing standard

Use this reference when drafting or revising LinkedIn posts, comments, replies, or employee advocacy copy.

## Evidence order

Build claims from, in order:

1. facts and language in the user's source material;
2. facts the user explicitly confirms;
3. clearly labeled assumptions that do not become claims.

Never invent a customer, quote, result, credential, event, product capability, or personal experience. Preserve attribution, material qualifications, and required disclosures. Ask for a missing fact when it is essential; otherwise omit the claim.

## Draft shape

- Give the post one audience, one job, and one central idea.
- Make the opening lines earn attention with specificity, tension, or a useful promise that the body fulfills.
- Put context before detail. Use short paragraphs and whitespace where it improves scanning, not as a rule after every sentence.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs to abstract business language.
- End when the idea is complete. Use a call to action only when it advances the stated objective.
- Use hashtags, emojis, mentions, and links only when the brief calls for them or they add information.

Do not assert a universal ideal length, posting time, hashtag count, or format. Those choices depend on the audience, account evidence, and current platform behavior.

## Voice controls

Translate broad voice labels into observable choices. For example, “direct and credible” can mean short claims, specific examples, few intensifiers, and no manufactured suspense. Keep the author's vocabulary and level of formality when source samples exist.

Avoid:

- engagement bait or false urgency;
- unsupported superlatives and predictions;
- throat-clearing such as “In today's fast-paced world”;
- stacked rhetorical questions;
- generic lessons detached from evidence;
- choppy line breaks used to imitate authenticity;
- “not X, but Y” and other repeated template constructions;
- claims that content is “human” merely because slang or typos were added.

## Final quality gate

Before returning a draft, verify:

- every factual statement has a supplied source;
- the hook and body make the same promise;
- the intended reader can tell why the idea matters;
- the language sounds plausible for the named author or brand;
- the response distinguishes final copy, variants, assumptions, and editorial notes;
- no publishing or scheduling action is implied.
